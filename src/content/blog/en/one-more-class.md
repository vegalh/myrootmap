“Other people’s children are already learning English and logic, and now there are AI classes too. Mine is three or four and isn’t enrolled in anything. Will they fall behind?”

It is hard not to wonder, especially when a class says your child can get an early start on something they will need. If I can afford to give them the chance, why not try?

“Falling behind” needs a second half: behind at what? A child who has taken a class may know the things done in that class better. If the worry is that the child will not learn on their own later, or will not know what to do with a problem they have never seen, then something else matters: in an ordinary day, do they get to start something themselves, try an idea, and see whether it worked?

Like the previous article, this one does not answer with my own opinion. It takes three questions, and for each says who did the research, what they saw and what it cannot tell us, before turning to how to use it at home. Every source is listed at the end and can be opened and checked.

Short on time? Read the short answer below and the three practices in section 3.

<div class="summary">
<p><strong>The short answer</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Wanting to add one more class is not just your own anxiety. When families have fewer children, each child receives more of the family’s investment in education; and the more education shapes a person’s future, the more reason parents have to invest. Both points have research behind them.</li>
<li>That research explains why parents feel the pressure. It does not say that one more class helps a three- or four-year-old.</li>
<li>In an experiment with four-year-olds, children who tried something with their own hands first and then watched an adult learned more accurately than children who watched first or only watched.</li>
<li>Parents who offer choices, listen to what the child thinks and explain the reasons for a rule tend to have children who put more of themselves into learning. At home, this can start with handing over one step of a job.</li>
</ul>

## 1. Why do I want to add a class when there is already too little time?

<p class="study"><strong>Research</strong>　National Bureau of Statistics of China, <em>Statistical Communiqué on the 2025 National Economic and Social Development</em>, 2026; Li, Zhang and Zhu (Chinese University of Hong Kong and others), <em>Demography</em>, 2008; Doepke (Northwestern University) and Zilibotti (Yale University), <em>Love, Money, and Parenting</em> (Princeton University Press, 2019) and <em>Econometrica</em>, 2017.</p>

**What they saw.** China’s National Bureau of Statistics reported 7.92 million births in 2025.<sup><a href="#ref-1">[1]</a></sup> The year before, the figure was 9.54 million.<sup><a href="#ref-7">[7]</a></sup>

When there are fewer children, does each one get more? Economists have studied this with Chinese census data. They compared families who had twins, and so one more child than they would otherwise have had, and found that the more children in a family, the fewer years of schooling each child received. The effect was stronger in rural areas, where public education was weaker.<sup><a href="#ref-2">[2]</a></sup> Read the other way, a family with fewer children has more to put into each one.

Doepke and Zilibotti explain the other half: why parents are willing to invest. Comparing countries and periods of history, they argue that when incomes are far apart and education matters a great deal for a person’s chances, parents have more reason to spend time and money on their children’s learning, and parenting becomes more intensive. In countries with smaller gaps, parents are generally more relaxed.<sup><a href="#ref-3">[3]</a></sup><sup><a href="#ref-4">[4]</a></sup>

Put together, these make “everyone else is doing it, shall we try too?” very hard to talk anyone out of in a sentence. What a parent usually fears is this: what if it really is a good chance, and I missed it on my child’s behalf?

**What this cannot tell us.** The number of births describes a population. It does not tell us how much expectation one child feels; that depends on how the adults take part in the child’s day. The twins study measured years of schooling in an earlier generation, not today’s after-school classes. And the economists explain why parents feel pressure. They did not test whether one more class helps a three- or four-year-old.

**How to use it at home.** Knowing where the pressure comes from makes it possible to separate “everyone else is enrolled” from “what does this class give my child?” A child who loves music, in a home with no instruments, can try something in a class that they cannot try otherwise. If the only reason left is that other children are doing it, look once more at the time the class would take: is there something in it that your child already wants to do and is always being hurried to finish?

## 2. Both are hands-on. Does it matter whether the child tries first or watches first?

<p class="study"><strong>Research</strong>　Sobel (Brown University) and Sommerville (University of Washington), <em>Frontiers in Psychology</em>, 2010.</p>

**What they saw.** The researchers asked 61 four-year-olds to work out a box with four buttons and four coloured lights. Some buttons lit several lights at once. The children were put into three groups, and at the end all were asked which button made which light come on. The box had two sets of rules, and every child learned both.<sup><a href="#ref-5">[5]</a></sup>

| How the child met the box | Correct, first set of rules | Correct, second set of rules |
|---|---|---|
| Tried it first, then watched an adult | 85% | 79% |
| Watched an adult first, then tried it | 71% | 66% |
| Only watched an adult | 69% | 60% |

The children who tried first did clearly better than those who only watched. Compared with those who watched and then tried, there was a difference too, but not a large enough one to rule out chance completely. Between watching then trying and only watching, no difference was found.

The children who tried first also pressed the buttons differently. They more often pressed one button at a time, and more often pressed the same button several times in a row. You can picture a child settling one very specific question: “Was it this button that lit that light? Let me press it again.”

**What this cannot tell us.** The experiment measured what four-year-olds understood about one light box, on the spot, in one session, with 61 children. It did not test other tasks or follow the children afterwards. The researchers themselves say it is not clear why trying first helped; the children may have been more engaged, or may have remembered better.

**How to use it at home.** When a child is working out how something works, and getting it wrong costs little, the first try can be left to them. An adult can still show how. When to show depends on what the child needs at that moment.

## 3. Where can I start at home if I want my child to take more initiative?

<p class="study"><strong>Research</strong>　Vasquez, Patall and colleagues, University of Texas at Austin, <em>Educational Psychology Review</em>, 2016.</p>

**What they saw.** Researchers use the term “autonomy support”. At home it means the adult is clear about what has to happen and where the limits are, and some of the deciding is still in the child’s hands. Going out today means wearing a coat; the adult decides that. Which coat, and whether to try putting it on alone first, can be the child’s decision.

Vasquez and colleagues brought together 36 studies. Where parents more often offered suitable choices, listened to what their children thought and explained the reasons for what they asked, children tended to do better at school, to put more of themselves into learning, and to have a better view of their own ability, a better attitude to school and better psychological health. The link with psychological health was the strongest.<sup><a href="#ref-6">[6]</a></sup>

**What this cannot tell us.** The 36 studies cover children from preschool to university and are not specifically about three- and four-year-olds. The authors state that every one of them only looked at whether two things go together, so they cannot show that what the parents did produced these results.

**How to use it at home.** It can be turned into one question you can see the answer to: in this job, which step can my child already take over? The three practices below follow from that question. No study has tested them. The first two list matching activities from the RootMap library, which give the materials, the steps and a safety note.

**Hand over part of breakfast.** Choose a weekend morning with no rush and put a banana, a bowl and a board where they can reach. The first time, they can peel the banana and share it out into bowls. When they enjoy it and their hands are steadier, they can try cutting a few pieces beside you with a child’s tool for soft food. How much to make and whom to serve first are theirs to decide. When it reaches the table, everyone eats the part they prepared.<br>*In RootMap:* [Cutting Bananas](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/cutting-bananas), [Orange Peeling](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/orange-peeling), [Egg Peeling and Cutting](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/egg-peeling-cutting).

**Turn “tidy your room” into a job with an end they can see.** For example, putting the picture books on the floor back on the low shelf. Clear one space on the shelf first so they know the books have somewhere to go. Put the first one back together and let them choose which to pick up next. Once this is familiar, ask them next time to look for themselves and see which books are still on the floor.<br>*In RootMap:* [Sorting Cutlery](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/sorting-cutlery), [Matching Socks](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/matching-socks), [Sweeping](https://vegalh.github.io/rootmapmvp/#/activity/sweeping).

**When they are building, let them change their own idea once first.** They want to build a garage a toy car can drive into, and find the door is too narrow. Wait while they push the car once and see whether they turn the car or move a block. If they come to you, hold one side steady and leave the choice of which block to move to them. When they push the car a second time, they can see for themselves whether the change worked.

## If you do one thing this week

Pick the moment when you most often say “never mind, I’ll do it”, and give yourself a few extra minutes. Let your child try first. If they are truly stuck, help with the step in front of them. The next time you do the same thing, see whether they can start by themselves, or tell you more exactly where they need your help.

## Back to that class

What research can tell us is this: with fewer children and more riding on education, parents’ wish to give a little more has real causes; four-year-olds who try for themselves first learn more accurately; and parents who leave room for choice tend to have children who take more initiative. What research does not tell us is which class is worth taking. That depends on what the class gives your child, and what it takes the place of.

When children do come to use AI, they will meet a similar question: there are several ways to go, so which one shall I try, and how will I know whether it worked? At three and four, some time can be kept for them to make one decision about a small thing they care about, see what happens, and decide what to do next.
